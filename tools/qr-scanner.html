What is a QR Code?

QR stands for "Quick Response." It is a type of two-dimensional matrix barcode that can store thousands of alphanumeric characters. Invented in 1994 by Masahiro Hara of Denso Wave (a Japanese automotive company), QR codes were originally used to track vehicles and parts during the manufacturing process. However, their incredible data density and fast readability soon made them popular for marketing, payments, and digital ticketing.

Unlike a standard barcode that only stores a small amount of data horizontally, a QR code stores data both horizontally and vertically. This allows it to hold up to 4,296 characters, which is enough to store a long URL, a full contact card (vCard), a secure WiFi password, or detailed text instructions.

Security Warning: Are QR Codes Safe to Scan?

While QR codes are incredibly useful, they are also a major security risk if used carelessly. Cybercriminals can create "Quishing" attacks (QR Code Phishing) by placing fake QR code stickers over legitimate ones on public posters, parking meters, or restaurant tables.

If you scan a malicious QR code, it might take you to a fake banking website designed to steal your password, or trigger a UPI payment to a fraudster.

How to stay safe:

  • Always Preview URLs: Our scanner does not auto-open links. It shows you the exact text or URL first. If the URL looks strange (e.g., a short link or a random domain), do not click it.
  • Check the Recipient Name: If you are scanning a UPI payment code, verify the recipient's name in the scanner result before opening your payment app.
  • Look for Tampering: If a QR code sticker in a public place looks like it has been placed on top of another sticker, it is highly suspicious.
  • Never enter passwords: If a QR code takes you to a login page, close it. Banks and legitimate services will never send you to a login page via a public QR code.

Troubleshooting: Why Won't My QR Code Scan?

  • Image Quality: If the image is too blurry, the pixels cannot be distinguished. Try a higher-resolution photo.
  • Damage: If the QR code is torn or heavily folded, the error correction might fail. (Remember, it can only handle up to 30% damage).
  • Glare: If you took a photo of a screen, a reflection might be covering the code. Take a new photo from a different angle.
  • Too Small: Ensure the QR code takes up most of the image frame. If the QR code is just a small part of a large photo, crop the image first and try again.
  • Inverted Colors: Sometimes QR codes are printed as white on black. Our scanner tries to detect these automatically, but if it fails, invert the colors of the image and try again.
