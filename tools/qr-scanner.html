What is a QR Code?

A QR code (short for "Quick Response code") is a two-dimensional barcode that stores data in a grid of black and white squares. Unlike a traditional linear barcode, which can only store a limited number of numeric characters, a QR code can hold up to 4,296 alphanumeric characters — enough to encode a long URL, a full contact card, a WiFi password, or a block of text.

QR codes were invented in 1994 by Masahiro Hara at Denso Wave, a Japanese automotive subsidiary. The original purpose was to track parts on a factory floor more efficiently than existing 1D barcodes. Hara's design was so effective that it spread far beyond manufacturing — by the 2010s, QR codes were used in marketing, payments, ticketing, and identity verification worldwide.

The 2020s saw explosive growth, especially in India where UPI payments made QR codes part of daily life. Today, over 2 billion QR codes are scanned every day globally.

How Do QR Codes Work?

A QR code is made up of several key parts:

  • Finder patterns: The three large squares in the corners that tell the scanner where the code begins and how it is oriented.
  • Alignment patterns: Smaller squares that help the scanner correct for perspective distortion.
  • Timing patterns: Dotted lines that help the scanner determine the size of each module.
  • Data modules: The actual black and white squares that encode the payload.
  • Error correction: Reed-Solomon error correction codes that allow the QR to still be readable even if up to 30% of it is damaged or hidden.
  • Quiet zone: The white border around the code that helps scanners isolate it from the background.

When you scan a QR code, the scanner software (in this case, the jsQR JavaScript library running inside your browser) analyses the image. It finds the three corner squares, determines the code's perspective, samples each module as black or white, and decodes the bit pattern back into text or a URL.

Types of QR Codes You Can Scan

QR codes are not all the same. They can store many types of data:

  • URL QR codes: Open a website, deep link, or app store link when scanned.
  • Text QR codes: Plain text messages — often used for notes, instructions, or quotes.
  • vCard QR codes: Store contact information (name, phone, email, company) in a single scan.
  • WiFi QR codes: Provide network SSID and password so devices can auto-connect.
  • Payment QR codes: Used by UPI, Paytm, PhonePe, Google Pay, and other payment systems.
  • Event ticket QR codes: Contain unique check-in tokens for concerts, flights, and conferences.
  • Email/SMS QR codes: Pre-fill an email or SMS draft when scanned.
  • GPS QR codes: Open a map location directly.
  • App QR codes: Link to an app in the Google Play Store or Apple App Store.

Improving Detection in Uploaded Images

Not every QR code photo is perfect. Blurry shots, poor lighting, and low-resolution screenshots can make decoding fail. DocEasy's scanner includes an automatic retry that helps in these cases:

  • Attempt 1: The tool decodes the image as-is, checking both normal and inverted (white-on-black) QR codes.
  • Attempt 2: If attempt 1 fails, the tool converts the image to high-contrast black-and-white and tries again. This fixes many glare, low-contrast, and colour-tint problems.

If both attempts fail, the QR code is likely too damaged, too small, or too blurry to decode. For best results:

  • Use a high-resolution screenshot or photograph.
  • Make sure the QR code fills most of the frame.
  • Avoid glare, shadows, blur, and rotation.
  • Crop the image closer to the QR before uploading.
  • If the QR is on a screen, increase the screen brightness and reduce glare.

Security Warning — Read This Before You Scan

QR codes are one of the easiest targets for scammers. Because a QR code is just a picture of a link or a payment address, a fraudster can stick a fake QR sticker over a legitimate one at a parking meter, restaurant table, or public poster. The victim scans it and unwittingly makes a payment to the scammer, or lands on a phishing site that looks like a bank login.

Follow these simple rules to stay safe:

  • Look before you scan: If the QR code sticker looks freshly applied or is on top of another sticker, treat it with suspicion.
  • Verify the payment recipient: When you scan a UPI or payment QR, always check the recipient name and amount shown inside your payment app before confirming.
  • Preview URLs first: Use a scanner like DocEasy to see the URL before opening it. Never open a QR link on your phone if it auto-opens into your banking app.
  • Beware of shortened links: If the QR opens a bit.ly, tinyurl, or other short link, be cautious. You cannot tell where it will take you until you click.
  • Never enter credentials on a page you reached from a QR: Banks and financial institutions never ask for passwords after a QR scan.
  • Use HTTPS: Legitimate URLs start with https://. If a QR opens a plain http:// page asking for personal data, close it immediately.

Technical Details (For Curious Readers)

Under the hood, DocEasy uses the open-source jsQR library, which implements full QR code decoding according to the ISO/IEC 18004 standard. It supports:

  • QR versions 1 through 40
  • All four error correction levels (L, M, Q, H)
  • Numeric, alphanumeric, byte, and Kanji encoding modes
  • Both normal and inverted QR codes
  • Micro QR codes and structured append

The library is loaded from a CDN and runs as pure JavaScript — no WebAssembly, no native plugins, no server dependency. It analyses image data through the Canvas API's getImageData method, converts it to black and white bit patterns using adaptive thresholding, locates the finder patterns, applies perspective correction, and then reads the data modules.

For the loud confirmation beep, DocEasy uses the Web Audio API. A short 880 Hz sine wave is generated dynamically when a QR code is detected — no external audio file is loaded, and the volume is set to maximum for clear feedback in noisy environments.

Tips for Best Scanning Results

  • Clean your camera lens: A smudged lens is the number one reason QR codes fail to scan.
  • Fill the frame: Point the camera so the QR code occupies at least 40% of the photo.
  • Hold steady: Motion blur prevents detection. Hold your phone still while taking the photo.
  • Avoid glare: Reflections on shiny surfaces ruin the black-and-white contrast that QR decoders rely on.
  • Good lighting: Bright, even light is ideal. Direct sunlight can wash out the QR; total darkness obviously fails.
  • Keep the QR flat: Curved or bent QR codes are hard to decode.
  • If the code is very small, move closer: Or upload a high-resolution photo.
