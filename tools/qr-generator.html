What is a QR Code?

A QR code (Quick Response code) is a two-dimensional matrix barcode invented in 1994 by Masahiro Hara at Denso Wave, a Japanese automotive subsidiary. Unlike traditional 1D barcodes that can only hold about 20 numeric characters, a QR code can store up to 4,296 alphanumeric characters or 7,089 numeric characters — enough to encode a long URL, a complete contact card, a WiFi password, or an entire block of text.

The distinctive square design is made up of:

  • Three finder patterns — the large corner squares that tell scanners where the code begins
  • Alignment patterns — smaller squares that help correct for perspective distortion
  • Timing patterns — dotted lines that define the grid size
  • Data modules — the actual payload encoded as black or white squares
  • Error correction codes — Reed-Solomon codes that allow up to 30% of the QR to be damaged and still scan reliably

Are Static QR Codes Better Than Dynamic QR Codes?

The QR code industry uses two types: static and dynamic.

Static QR codes (which DocEasy generates) encode the actual content directly into the black-and-white square pattern. This means:

  • They never expire
  • They work offline — the scanner doesn't need an internet lookup
  • No third-party server is involved in the scan
  • Zero ongoing cost
  • No tracking of who scanned the code

Dynamic QR codes (used by paid platforms) encode a short redirect URL. When scanned, the phone hits the vendor's server, which then forwards the user to the real destination. This allows the vendor to:

  • Change the destination without reprinting the QR
  • Track scan analytics
  • Disable the QR when the subscription ends

Dynamic QR codes are useful for large marketing campaigns, but they also mean your code stops working the moment you stop paying, and every scan passes through the vendor's server.

For most personal, small-business, and Indian UPI use cases, static QR codes are strictly better — and that's what DocEasy gives you.

Best Practices for Printing QR Codes

Generating a QR code is easy, but a bad print job can make it unscannable. Follow these rules for guaranteed results:

  • Minimum physical size: Never print a QR code smaller than 2 × 2 cm (0.8 × 0.8 inches). Below that, many phone cameras struggle to focus.
  • Ideal scan distance: A rule of thumb — the QR code side should be at least 1/10th of the scanning distance. For 1 metre away, print at least 10 cm × 10 cm.
  • High contrast: Always place the QR code on a white or light background. Never invert to white-on-black — most scanners fail on inverted codes.
  • Quiet zone: Leave at least 4 modules of white space around the QR code. Don't let text or images touch the edges.
  • Good lighting: Avoid shiny, glossy paper if it reflects light. Matte finishes work best for scanning.
  • Test before printing thousands: Always print one copy and scan it with multiple phones before placing a large order.
  • Use 1000px for print: For brochures and posters, choose the 1000px download option so the QR stays sharp when scaled up.

QR Code Security — Stay Safe

QR codes are convenient, but they have also been misused by scammers. Follow these safety practices:

  • Preview the destination: Before scanning an unknown QR code, use our QR Code Scanner to see what's inside.
  • Look for tampering: If a QR sticker looks pasted on top of another, treat it with suspicion.
  • Verify UPI recipient: Always check the payee name shown inside your payment app before confirming.
  • Avoid public WiFi QR codes that ask for banking credentials.
  • Never scan a QR from an unsolicited email or message — that's the most common phishing tactic.
