How do I build a professional resume?

A strong resume has 5 key sections: (1) Header: Name, phone, email, LinkedIn, and city. (2) Summary: 2–3 lines describing your experience and target role. (3) Experience: Reverse-chronological list with company, role, dates, and 3–5 bullet points per role showing impact (quantified where possible — "increased sales by 25%"). (4) Skills: 8–12 relevant technical and soft skills. (5) Education: Degrees, certifications, and relevant coursework. Keep it to 1 page for under 10 years of experience, 2 pages maximum otherwise. Use a clean, ATS-friendly format (no tables, no columns, no graphics). DocEasy's upcoming Resume Builder will generate this structure automatically from your inputs and export as PDF.

What is an ATS and why does it matter?

ATS (Applicant Tracking System) is software that companies use to screen resumes before a human ever sees them. About 75% of mid-to-large companies (and nearly all Fortune 500 companies) use ATS like Workday, Taleo, Greenhouse, or Lever. When you apply online, your resume is parsed by the ATS, and only resumes that match the job description's keywords proceed. ATS-unfriendly resumes — with tables, images, unusual fonts, or missing keywords — get auto-rejected. DocEasy's upcoming ATS Resume Checker will scan your resume against a specific job description and score it on keyword match, formatting, and readability, so you can fix issues before applying.

What is the STAR method for interviews?

STAR stands for Situation, Task, Action, Result — a structured way to answer behavioral interview questions like "Tell me about a time when you…". Situation: Describe the context (1–2 sentences). Task: Explain what you needed to accomplish (1 sentence). Action: Describe the specific steps you took (2–4 sentences, focus on "I" not "we"). Result: Share the measurable outcome (1–2 sentences with numbers if possible). For example: "Our team's onboarding time was 3 weeks (Situation). I was asked to reduce it to under 10 days (Task). I redesigned the checklist, automated 5 manual steps, and created a mentor program (Action). Onboarding dropped to 7 days, saving 40 hours per new hire (Result)." DocEasy's STAR Answer Builder will guide you through this structure for each interview answer.

How do I negotiate a higher salary?

Successful negotiation follows 5 steps: (1) Research market rate: Use Glassdoor, AmbitionBox, Levels.fyi, or DocEasy's Salary Comparison (coming soon) to know the range for your role, city, and experience. (2) Anchor high: When asked for expectations, give a range with your target at the bottom. Say "I'm looking at ₹18–22 lakh based on my research." (3) Justify with value: Frame it as ROI — "I've increased revenue 30% at my last role, and I plan to do the same here." (4) Be prepared to walk: The strongest negotiators have multiple offers. (5) Consider total comp: Base salary, bonus, equity, sign-on, relocation, and benefits. A ₹2 lakh higher base is worth more than a ₹3 lakh bonus that's discretionary. Never accept the first offer — almost every company has room to negotiate 5–15%.

Which is more important — resume or cover letter?

For most roles, the resume is more important. Recruiters spend 6–8 seconds scanning a resume before deciding whether to read further — and many never read the cover letter at all. That said, cover letters still matter for: (1) Senior roles (Manager+), where judgment and communication matter; (2) Career changes, where you need to explain why you're pivoting; (3) Small companies where the founder reads every application; (4) Creative roles where writing style is part of the job. For most applications: perfect the resume first, then write a short (150–250 word) customized cover letter as a bonus. Never send a generic cover letter — that's worse than none.

How do I explain a career gap?

Career gaps are more common than ever (post-COVID, sabbaticals, caregiving, upskilling). The formula: be honest, brief, and forward-looking. Never apologize or over-explain. Examples: "I took 8 months off to care for a family member, and I'm now ready to return full-time." "I spent 6 months completing a data science certification and building 3 portfolio projects." "I took a planned sabbatical to travel and reflect, and I'm excited to re-enter the workforce." On your resume, fill the gap with the honest label ("Career Break — Travel") rather than hiding it, which looks worse when discovered. In interviews, spend 10 seconds on the gap and the rest on why you're the right fit now.

How many pages should a resume be?

Rules of thumb: Fresher (0–2 years): 1 page. Mid-level (3–8 years): 1 page, extend to 2 only if you have significant achievements to showcase. Senior (8–15 years): 2 pages. Executive (15+ years): 2–3 pages (or a 2-page resume + separate accomplishments addendum). Never pad a resume with irrelevant experience to fill pages — recruiters see through it. If you're struggling to fit on 1 page, cut older roles (pre-2015 can be a single line), remove outdated skills, and tighten bullet points. If you have too much and can't cut, use a clean 2-page layout with consistent formatting. Remember: a strong 1-page resume beats a weak 2-page resume every time.

Can I create different resumes for different jobs?

Yes — you should. Tailoring your resume for each role significantly increases callback rates. The formula: keep a "master resume" with everything (all roles, all skills, all achievements), then create a tailored version for each application by selecting the most relevant 80% and adding keywords from the job description. This takes 10–15 minutes per application but can double your response rate. The upcoming Resume Builder will support multiple resume versions — save your master, then duplicate and customize for each job. Different industries (tech vs. finance vs. marketing) also warrant different resume styles.

How do I know if my resume is good enough?

Three ways to evaluate: (1) Feedback loop: If you're applying to 20+ jobs and getting zero callbacks, your resume needs work. A good resume gets 5–15% callback rate. (2) Peer review: Show it to 3 people in your industry (not just friends) and ask "What 3 things would you change?" (3) ATS score: Run it through an ATS checker (DocEasy's is coming soon) against a specific job description — aim for 75%+ keyword match. Red flags: typos (kills 60% of resumes), vague bullets ("responsible for..."), no numbers/metrics, more than 2 pages, dense walls of text, or missing contact info. If any of these apply, fix them before applying.

Are the salary benchmarks accurate?

Salary benchmarks vary by source, and no single source is perfect. Most salary sites (Glassdoor, AmbitionBox, Levels.fyi, PayScale) rely on user-submitted data, which can be skewed by self-selection bias — people with higher salaries are more likely to report. DocEasy's Salary Comparison tool (coming soon) will aggregate public data from multiple sources and show you a range (min, median, max) rather than a single number. Treat it as a starting point for negotiation, not a definitive answer. For the most accurate number, combine: (1) Data from salary sites; (2) Conversations with peers in similar roles; (3) The company's own pay bands (if they publish them).

How often should I update my resume?

At minimum: every 6 months, even if you're not job hunting. Here's why: (1) You'll forget specific achievements if you wait too long — write them down while fresh; (2) Annual reviews and project completions are worth capturing; (3) Having an up-to-date resume makes it easy to apply quickly when opportunities arise; (4) Your resume grows stale as your skills and experience grow. Best practice: after every major project or accomplishment, add a bullet point to your "master resume". Then, when you actually need to apply, tailoring for a specific role takes 10 minutes instead of 3 hours.
